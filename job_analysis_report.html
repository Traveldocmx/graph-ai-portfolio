Job Posting Analysis Report

Introduction

This project shows job market data that was scraped from the web. The aim of this project is to evaluate the years of experience and the languages with more demand on the job market, and to correlate that with average salary. This could help tailor what we teach in our courses to provide students with more demanded skills.

Analysis of Years of Experience

Visualization: Experience vs. Salary

Interpretation

This graph shows the correlation between minimum years of experience and salary. On the X axis we can see the minimum years of experience and on the Y axis, the median salary. The red line indicates the linear regression fit, and the pink shaded area represents the uncertainty band (95% CI). The number β = 664.25 indicates the slope.

The graph shows that for each additional year of minimum experience, the median salary increases by $664 on average, which is really not a big difference. My interpretation is that there is not a strong linear relationship between years of experience and salary. In other words, salaries vary a lot across different years.

Analysis of Programming Language Requirements

Visualization: Salary by Data Language

Interpretation

This is a box plot. The horizontal line inside the boxes represents the salary median. The top and bottom borders represent the interquartile range (25%-75%). The dots are extreme values or outliers.

On the x axis we can see four programming language categories (Both, Neither, Python, or R). On first glance, the median salaries look similar across all four boxes, at around 60-70k, although some higher paying jobs may require both languages. R shows the lowest value but is not greatly smaller. There are outliers, which means there are high paying jobs that do not necessarily consider only language programming skills.

Reflection

The process was really easy. It is incredible how fast I was able to clean the data on Google Sheets using the API and to analyze the data in Cursor. I actually think the most cumbersome process is uploading everything to GitHub.

To me, the greatest limitation is that we are limiting the analysis to just two factors: years of experience and programming language, to just two programming languages.

Another limitation is the balance of the data regarding programming languages, which is a direct function of the reduced sample size. Although 400 job descriptions seem like a lot, when data is split into groups, we end up with small groups which can produce noisy data. The power to detect important differences in this dataset may be limited by its sample size.

Only 10 jobs mentioned R as the only language, in comparison to Python (mentioned by 48 jobs) and Neither (190 jobs). Smaller groups may cause noise in the data and imprecise results.

Finally, at this point I am not able to see if there are any duplicates in the data, which could be done probably by using the API in Google Sheets.

Conclusions

The data shows that the market is complex and high paying positions require more than R and/or Python.

A few recommendations would be to:

  1. Focus on versatility to train students who can adapt to different tool ecosystems.

  2. Other tools that may drive salaries higher could be SQL, data visualization, and business intelligence tools.

  3. The weak experience-salary correlation suggests that demonstrable skills and project portfolio may matter more than years of experience.
